Legislation • High YieldPrimary Source: Health and Disability Commissioner (HDC) — Code of Rights

HDC Code of Consumers’ Rights (10 Rights Reference)

State Final scenarios frequently test Rights 1–10 in clinical priority decisions, especially Right 5 (Communication/Interpreters), Right 7 (Informed Consent & Refusal), and Right 10 (Complaints).

  • •Right 1: Right to be treated with respect (includes cultural & religious values / Tikanga Māori).
  • •Right 2: Right to freedom from discrimination, coercion, harassment, and exploitation.
  • •Right 3: Right to dignity and independence.
  • •Right 4: Right to services of an appropriate clinical, ethical, and legal standard.
  • •Right 5: Right to effective communication (use a competent interpreter where necessary; avoid relying on minors or family for consent discussions).
  • •Right 6: Right to be fully informed (diagnosis, options, risks, expected outcomes).
  • •Right 7: Right to make an informed choice and give informed consent (includes right to refuse or withdraw consent at any time, and Right 7(9) return of body parts/whenua).
  • •Right 8: Right to have one or more support persons / whānau present.
  • •Right 9: Rights in respect of teaching or research (explicit consent required for student involvement).
  • •Right 10: Right to complain (facilitate resolution and provide contact details for independent Health & Disability Advocates).

Standards • NCNZ RN six pouPrimary Source: NCNZ — Standards of competence for registered nurses 2025

The six pou: NCNZ standards of competence for registered nurses

The Nursing Council of New Zealand (NCNZ) registered nurse standards of competence (document dated February 2025; in effect from 1 April 2025 for continuing competence; for all purposes from 1 January 2026) are organised into six pou, each with its own descriptors. Te Tiriti o Waitangi, kawa whakaruruhau and cultural safety run through them.

  • •Pou one: Māori health — a commitment to Māori health: nurses support, respect and protect Māori rights, advocate for equitable outcomes, and address power imbalances while working collaboratively with Māori.
  • •Pou two: Cultural safety — culturally safe care is determined by the recipient of care (descriptor 2.1); nurses reflect on their practice and cultural identity, and challenge racism and discrimination (descriptor 2.2).
  • •Pou three: Whanaungatanga and communication — building relationships through effective, culturally appropriate communication, including with the interprofessional team.
  • •Pou four: Pūkengatanga and evidence-informed nursing practice — clinical skills and critical thinking informed by current evidence to provide safe, quality care: assess, plan, intervene, and evaluate.
  • •Pou five: Manaakitanga and people-centred care — compassion, collaboration and partnership that build trust with people, whānau or communities and support shared decision-making.
  • •Pou six: Rangatiratanga and leadership — acting as a change agent, speaking out and escalating concerns on behalf of colleagues or recipients of care; professional and ethical accountability (descriptor 6.2).
  • •Tapu & Noa in Ward Care: Where possible, avoid placing items used for bodily waste (bedpans/urinals) on food tables, and ask the person or whānau about their preferences; keep head pillows (tapu) separate from cushions used for legs/feet.

Professional Practice • DelegationPrimary Source: NCNZ — FAQs changes to direction and delegation 2025

RN Direction & Delegation to Enrolled Nurses (ENs) and HCAs

Knowing what must be retained by the RN versus what can be delegated to an Enrolled Nurse or Healthcare Assistant is a classic State Final clinical priority topic.

  • •RN Accountability: The delegating RN retains professional accountability for assessing the patient, verifying the competence of the person receiving delegation, and providing appropriate clinical direction.
  • •Registered Nurse (RN) Exclusive Responsibilities: Initial comprehensive admission assessment, care planning for complex/unstable patients, and titration of high-risk intravenous infusions (e.g., IV insulin, inotropes).
  • •Enrolled Nurses (EN): Regulated practitioners who practise under the direction and delegation of an RN (or nurse practitioner) for patients with predictable health outcomes.
  • •Healthcare Assistants (HCA): Unregulated healthcare workers; may perform delegated routine tasks for stable patients (hygiene, mobility, routine vitals if trained), but clinical assessment, triage and medication/IV administration are not appropriate to delegate to an HCA; follow the HPCA Act scope-of-practice rules and the employer delegation policy.
  • •Five Rights of Delegation: Right task, Right circumstances, Right person, Right direction/communication, Right supervision and evaluation.

Statutory Law • HPCA Act 2003Primary Source: New Zealand Legislation — HPCA Act 2003

Health Practitioners Competence Assurance Act 2003 (HPCA Act)

The overarching statute governing all regulated health professions in New Zealand. Its primary purpose (Section 3) is to protect the health and safety of members of the public.

  • •Primary Purpose (Section 3): To protect the health and safety of members of the public by providing mechanisms to ensure health practitioners are competent and fit to practise.
  • •Protected Titles & Scopes of Practice (Section 7 & 8): Only practitioners registered with the Nursing Council and holding a current Annual Practising Certificate (APC) may use the title Registered Nurse (RN) or Enrolled Nurse (EN).
  • •Continuing Competence (Recertification / PDRP): NCNZ requires RNs to maintain a current APC, complete at least 450 practice hours and 60 hours of professional development every 3 years, and demonstrate competence against the six pou.
  • •Fitness to Practise Notification (Section 45): Health practitioners have a statutory obligation to notify the Registrar if they have reason to believe another practitioner is unable to perform required functions safely due to mental or physical condition (including substance impairment).

Statutory Law • Mental Health Act 1992Primary Source: New Zealand Legislation — Mental Health Act 1992

Mental Health (Compulsory Assessment and Treatment) Act 1992

Key statutory powers and patient rights when caring for acutely unwell patients requiring urgent psychiatric assessment, including the RN 6-hour holding power (Section 111).

  • •Statutory Definition of Mental Disorder (Section 2): An abnormal state of mind (continuous or intermittent) characterised by delusions, or disorders of mood, perception, volition, or cognition, to such a degree that it poses a serious danger to the health or safety of that person or others, or seriously diminishes self-care capacity.
  • •Section 111 — Registered Nurse’s Holding Power: If a voluntary inpatient appears to be mentally disordered and tries to leave, an RN may detain the patient for up to 6 HOURS for the purpose of enabling an urgent medical examination, and must immediately contact a Duly Authorised Officer (DAO) / medical practitioner.
  • •Duly Authorised Officer (DAO): A designated mental health professional who coordinates statutory assessments, advises whānau, and arranges Section 8A/8B medical examinations.
  • •Patient Rights under Part 6 (Sections 64–75): Compulsory status does NOT remove general rights—patients retain the right to legal advice, cultural support/kaumātua, company, to receive visitors/phone calls (unless formally restricted by the Responsible Clinician for clinical safety), and review by the Mental Health Review Tribunal.
